# Model and evidence notes

## What is measured

This is a research simulation using real 2017 grocery baskets, not a reconstruction or validation of an Instacart warehouse. Order lines are the unit of pick work; the data do not provide quantities. Each repeated SKU across batched orders incurs one pick service per order line. No seasonal inference is made because calendar dates are absent. Day-of-week/hour fields describe shopping activity, not one facility's arrival rate.

All velocity ranks, the 90% cutoff and storage assignments use the 80% training split of prior orders only. A stable SplitMix64 hash of order_id with salt 20261027 assigns about 20% to holdout. Affinities use a fixed uniform sample of 120,000 training orders. Evaluation uses a disjoint uniform sample of 60,000 held-out orders. Complete orders touching any excluded SKU are excluded, never silently shortened. The resulting order-selection bias is quantified in scope_summary.csv. Order-level splitting does not isolate users; results concern new orders from this population, not unseen customers.

## Geometry and storage

The compact scope uses the top 800 training SKUs in 10 aisles. Temperature block rounding requires 46 slots per side (920 available positions), rather than the starter 40. The 90% line-coverage scope uses 9,149 SKUs in 115 aisles, each with 40 positions per side (9,200 positions). Zone blocks are ordered ambient, chilled, frozen from the depot. Aisle positions are x = 4(a+1) meters; pick depths are y = 1,...,S meters. Cross aisles are at y = 0 and S+1. The depot is (0,0). One-meter slot pitch, four-meter aisle-center spacing and 2.5-meter nominal clear width are design assumptions, not data-derived measurements. No middle cross aisle, rack height, cube, replenishment or vehicle capacity is modeled.

Temperature is a department-level proxy: frozen is frozen; dairy eggs, meat seafood, deli and produce are chilled; everything else is ambient. In particular, real produce and beverages can need finer classification. This mapping is not a food-safety determination. Frozen picks are last; chilling exposure time and cold-chain feasibility are not optimized.

ABC fills the nearest aisle first within each zone, in descending velocity. Random is a single fixed within-zone assignment (seed 301). Spread distributes descending-velocity SKUs across all aisle fronts before using deeper positions. Affinity greedily follows the strongest still-unassigned same-zone co-pick neighbor, starting each disconnected chain from the highest remaining velocity; consecutive products occupy adjacent rack slots. It is a heuristic, not a proven optimizer. A50 and A100 restrict the A-class (80% of zone training lines) to 50% or 100% of each zone's aisles, subject to enough slots, balancing those items across fronts. Remaining products fill depth-first across aisles. Thus A100 differs from full Spread in the placement of B/C items.

## Routing

Both heuristics use a separate closed sub-tour for each visited temperature block, beginning/ending at that block's leftmost visited front aisle. Blocks are processed in temperature order and linked along the front cross aisle. This conservative zone-wise construction adds travel relative to a globally optimized tour. It is deliberately held fixed across policies.

S-shape traverses pairs of visited aisles; an unpaired last aisle is entered and returned from the front to its deepest pick. Largest-gap traverses the first and last visited aisles fully; in interior aisles it skips the largest gap between consecutive pick depths, including the front/back boundary gaps. It serves remaining picks through return excursions from front or rear. With only one visited aisle, it returns from the front. Both sides' picks at the same depth are combined into a single physical stop, with service proportional to lines. Independent distance formulas sum horizontal zone sub-tours and vertical traversals; the simulation sums actual unit-cell movement.

## Blocking and traffic control

The picking corridor is one shared lane: a picker at a depth blocks access past that depth on both rack sides. This is more conservative than modeling independent lanes on two sides of a 2.5-meter aisle. Both rack faces retain separate storage slots. Passing is allowed on cross aisles and at the depot, where there is no congestion resource. Consequently, this is not an exact recreation of the circular no-passing model in Gue et al.

Every depth is a capacity-one occupancy cell. A picker requests the next cell while holding the current one, releases the current cell when the next is granted, and occupies the next during one pitch of travel and any pick service. Same-direction full traversals can share an aisle without overtaking. A FIFO directional gate blocks head-on entries; once opposite traffic is queued, it cannot be starved by later arrivals. A return excursion reserves the aisle exclusively until the U-turn is completed. This conservative dispatch rule avoids impossible head-on swaps but contributes to measured blocking. Blocking therefore includes both entry-control waiting and waiting behind a picker; it is not solely local pick-face waiting. The largest-gap sensitivity changes the frequency of exclusive returns, so its congestion differences are not pure distance effects.

The fast simulator books deterministic cell-release times upon admission. With no lateral entry and compatible direction, these release times are exact; there is no speed approximation. The retained simulation_reference.py instead uses a SimPy Resource per cell. Differential tests compare every returned metric across 24 combinations of routing, staffing, passing and arrivals. Both are discrete-event simulations; the optimized implementation processes one timeout per aisle visit instead of several per cell.

Passing-allowed sensitivity removes both cell conflicts and the direction gate, but keeps identical routes and pick times. It is an optimistic no-interference bound, not a calibrated wide-aisle model with residual same-location queues. No-passing at 2.5 meters is an operating/cart assumption; aisle width alone does not establish it.

## Time and demand

Base service assumptions: 1.2 m/s walking, 15 s per order line, and 30 s depot handling per tour. Gue et al. discuss pick-to-walk ratios and give an illustrative experiment with 20 s picks and 2 s travel per point; this study's dimensional choices are engineering assumptions, not parameters estimated from that paper or Instacart. Sensitivities vary picks to 10/20 s and walking to 1.0/1.3 m/s.

Capacity experiments keep work available, allowing operating curves to measure achievable model throughput rather than flatten at an arbitrary demand cap. Separate demand experiments use Poisson order arrivals, FCFS batching, and a common rate across policies. Full batches wait until k orders accumulate; no timeout or partial-batch flush is used. A pre-sampled independent held-out basket sequence supplies batch contents. Each replication uses common random numbers across policies and staffing (order-draw seed 10000+rep; arrival seed 20000+rep). The stream has 4,096 batches; recycling is flagged and checked in results.

Without an actual warehouse demand, the central scenario is 70% of eight-picker ABC uncongested capacity estimated from held-out batches. This is a deliberately calibrated scenario, not observed demand. The same rate is held constant as staffing changes. Understaffed demand runs accumulate backlog and are not interpreted as steady-state queues. Saturated runs have utilization 100% by construction; demand-run utilization is reported separately. Additional demand multipliers are capacity-planning scenarios unless separately simulated.

Each run starts empty, has a one-hour warm-up followed by an eight-hour measurement window, and does not drain unfinished tours. A two-hour warm-up sensitivity checks compact-scope capacity at 8 and 20 pickers. Counts include all tours completed in the measurement window, even if begun during warm-up. Labor-state totals clip every state interval to the window, including unfinished tours. Mean tour duration uses completed tours and can have finite-horizon selection effects. Queue time is ready-batch-to-picker-start, and oldest-order duration includes batching delay. Paid shift time includes idle and blocking time. WIP includes unbatched orders, queued batches and active tours; flow conservation is checked in every demand run.

## Statistics and decision rule

Stage 1 uses all eligible baskets in the fixed evaluation sample, with order-level t intervals for mean travel and paired differences. These intervals are conditional on the population of eligible orders, fixed storage assignments and geometry; they do not capture customer clustering or layout estimation uncertainty. Stage 3 has ten independently seeded replications per cell. Intervals use Student t, not normal 1.96, and within-replication paired ABC/Spread differences. The main crossover audit includes Bonferroni simultaneous 95% intervals over the eight tested staffing levels within each layout. A linearly interpolated crossing is descriptive only; the first tested sustained advantage is a separate field. Absence of a crossing through 20 is not proof that no crossing exists.

Labor hours per 1,000 completed orders = 1,000 * pickers * 8 / completed orders during the eight-hour window. Capacity-based staffing selects the smallest tested picker count whose lower 95% throughput bound is at least demand / 0.85. This reserves nominal 15% headroom; it is not a service-level guarantee. At demand, nominal paid labor cost is 1,000 * pickers / demand. The illustrative $25/hour wage is not a market wage estimate. Capital, refrigeration, slot relocation, replenishment, supervision, breaks, errors and tail-order handling are outside the cost boundary. Thus a recommendation applies only to the modeled eligible workload.
